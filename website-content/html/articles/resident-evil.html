[Sinister Voice] Resident Evil

Written 06/19/26

Gameplay

From the first zombie encounter, Resident Evil enforces its core concept of risk versus reward. You can run from the zombie, or you can try and get the ammo behind it. This system is apparent to any player after a couple minutes of gameplay, but a player may be shocked to realize that behind a visible blocky and relatively "clunky" game, exists a robust and elegant system.

Fight or flight is a surface level view, each option can be further broken down into further risk/reward choices. While avoiding zombies you can attempt to run past and maneuver around the them, or you could use your resources and shoot at a zombie to briefly stun them. During a fight you can use your best resources, or you can be patient and drag out a fight with a numerous Beretta shots and knife stabs.

After receiving a shotgun, the player may start to realize just how important timing is, and as such another risk/reward scenario reveals itself: stand still and time your shot to shoot off a zombie's head, or miss your shot and be bitten up.

The headshot timing is what makes the control system really start to come together, it's fun. The tank controls were not designed for "run and gun" gameplay, they work best when the player stands in one place and line up shots. There are a few rooms where the game does push the player to run around tight corners (Chimera control room...), and in those moments the controls are cumbersome, but otherwise they serve their purpose exceedingly well. More than anything, they were needed to support the fully rendered backgrounds and cinematic angles that give Resident Evil its entire character.

Cinematic framing during gameplay is difficult to implement. We come to a fundamental divide on how game design should function: should gameplay be seamless and (somewhat) mindless, or should gameplay have rough edges if it supports a unique experience. The safest bet is simple controls, but as we enter an era where nearly all games play the same, unique control schemes help make games stand out.

The gameplay of Resident Evil often reminded me of Killer7, I would maybe argue that Killer7 is the perfect answer on how to control a game such that it still allows for cinematic control over the camera. The minute-to-minute grunt battles of these games will escape my memory, but I will always remember how these games looked at even the most insignificant moments.

Of course, most importantly, Resident Evil is a horror game, and horror is built on surprises. Resident Evil seldom needs 'in your face' jump scares, the player can simply advance forward and witness the camera shift to reveal an enemy just around the corner.

Puzzles and Structure

Cutely, the cinematics are also used to push the player forward during the puzzles. The player's eyes are controlled and guided to necessary set pieces from the minute they enter the room.

The puzzles overall were decent, none that were extremely exciting and rewarding, but none too egregious. The only one that I felt truly cheated by was the "lion gem" puzzle. The set up for the puzzle led me to believe I needed both gems to progress, but each gem gives a different reward. The rewards are unrelated as well, so it's very odd to combine them together in a puzzle (I actually had to look up the solution since I had had check all the rooms, but could not figure out where the wind emblem was).

It's not really a puzzle, but I found the first Yawn segment a bit odd on multiple fronts, at least in the Jill playthrough. It felt like a needless obstacle to get a few more precious minutes of runtime. You cannot pick up the serum from the storeroom until Richard asks you to get it. As soon as you return to him, he dies. The whole purpose of this segment then, is to introduce the serum and serve it to the player, but the serum only exists in relation to Yawn.

On a zoomed out note, Yawn is the enemy that introduces poison to the player, but it operates on an entirely different system than all other poison enemies. Resident Evil was typically very skilled at introducing concepts before throwing them at the player in gameplay, which makes Yawn a bit odd.

Jill and Chris

As stated, I would not change how the game plays, but there were points where difficulty tipped over into annoyance. I completed the game with Jill first, but I wanted to be thorough so I did a Chris playthrough after. To be honest, this playthrough was less fun and not for reasons I would have expected.

I had already gone through the game once, so I was much braver. Once that paranoia was gone and all the zombies and rooms were known, I realized that half of the gameplay, for me, worked because I was scared. I was too afraid to run past zombies so I would fight or find a safe, but tedious, avenue to escape.

As Chris, I was able to get through the mansion, guardhouse, and second mansion part with very few troubles, and quickly as well. To be fair, I do think I damaged my own enjoyment since I did my run in one sitting and was trying to see how fast I could get through it. It was not a complete speedrun, I did open up nearly every door I came across even if I knew it didn't move the major puzzles forward.

The combat is great when its slow and methodical, but it is important for a game to have variations. I understand why Resident Evil's back half features quicker enemies, but in practice they were more aggrivating than fun. Being stun-locked by hoards of dogs and hunters was annoying. A few rooms in the Chris playthrough could have stood to have less enemies in extremely tight corridors.

To be honest, at the end of the catacombs I decided it would be best to stop there since I already knew how everything plays out (not just for Jill, I had seen Chris's ending) and the fun was fading, but I ended up using cheats and save states so I could at least see the ending that was only a few screens away.

The "hard" mode of Resident Evil pulled away the curtain and some of the magic was gone. The game seemed to work best when I was a bit of a wimp, but as Chris I always felt empowered. When I did not, I did not feel weak, I felt annoyed. In a strange way Rebecca's existence partially tampers the tone. I like her, and that's the issue. When playing as Jill, Barry was often nebulous. Here and out.

Rebecca was much more present and I felt like I was protecting her, which made everything less scary. At its height, it is stressful when she is cornered by a hunter, but that is about it. Chris's larger health pool, and Rebecca's healing capabilities only compounded this feeling. I originally wanted to argue that, although the different benefits for different character's make sense, Jill gaining more ammo and Chris gaining more health, in practice they feel much more slanted to Chris's favor, but I did stop my Chris playthrough with very few healing items and ammo, so maybe that's an incorrect inference.

My gameplay as Chris was effected by it being a second run, but Chris feels designed to be so. Some of the fat is trimmed (Yawn), and the game is much more difficult, as such will be most possible with familiarity of the mansion.

The protagonist system is interesting here, but I think it is better done in Resident Evil 2 where the stories are designed to coexist. Obviously, this is the first game and they are just testing concepts out, but it does feel weird how the character selection results in different realities.

Also, as you might expect, I'm never much a fan of a character's gender leading to gameplay health differences, but personally, the game painted Jill as being much more competent and strong compared to Chris. She can play the piano, mix chemicals, master lockpicking, and use a grenade launcher? I love you Jill.

Story

Just as the presumably "dated" controls and graphics hide a well designed system, the poor voice acting a dialogue makes the honestly clever story presentation a surprise.

It is often true that explaining everything ruins the magic, but Resident Evil is a great case for how explaining every little detail can turn a story on its head. The dilapidated lock-key mansion and Halloween zombies all melt away to reveal calculated corporate intent. Every monster serves its purpose, and even the player themselves is fulfilling a task. (I think Noah Caldwell-Gervais describes this best in their review).

I was not around, but from playing other iconic PS1 titles, I get the feeling that the PS1 was an era for exploration. Tropes had been developed and expectations were created, so games could start playing with these concepts. Party member betrayal is oft used, and is oft very exciting, but its hard to explain away why an enemy would place the player in the perfect position to claim victory. Resident Evil is able to mostly avoid these strange narrative explanations, but it is less exciting.

Metal Gear Solid had a twist betrayal that left many plot holes, but it worked well because Metal Gear Solid has great character writing. I did not care about Wesker's betrayal because I do not care about Wesker (He does make me laugh though).

The ending of the game also get's a little wonky with Wesker's motivation. It's strange to build up Umbrella so well only to have Wesker do his own thing and betray them as well in the end. I like that Umbrella looms over the story, but this "twist" only tampers Umbrella's and Wesker's execution.

This might be a weird complaint, but I wish that Resident Evil was a bit more reactive to the player. As mentioned, the first zombie encounter in the game serves a strong mechanical introduction, but no matter what you do the same cutscene of the zombie rushing out of the room will follow (I do believe that the cutscene won't play if you kill the zombie as Chris). There are different routes and endings, which is neat, but I feel like just a bit more detail could be beneficial

There are many moving pieces and characters in the plot, but the player never has a chance to stumble on any piece in a transitional action. If a character is not present, they do not exist. I think this was apparent to the writers though because Resident Evil 2 is very interested in managing simultaneously existing stories, as we discussed earlier in regards to the character selection.

On a final note, I genuinely love the voice acting. I think if you have uninteresting characters and bland dialogue the best thing you can do is find the worst voice actors you can. Robot Alchemic Drive characters are not beloved for their writing, they are beloved because you will be repeating their voice intonations for months to come.

In Resident Evil's case, bland isn't even the right word to describe the dialogue quality. The translation is poor in an enticing way. The characters talk like a virtual buddy that exists to "subtly" cram in text from the game's manual ("Master of Unlocking"). It is addictive. I would love to be able to intentionally write dialogue in that style.
