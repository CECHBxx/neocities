Hotel World

Written 10/14/26

Story

Hotel Dusk: Room 215 requires the player to hold the DS vertically. Despite being a gimmick, the orientation crafts the experience and guides the player to view the game as a novel. The game packs symbols and parallels into every character and situation in a novelistic manner (NoteStart: All media uses literary devices, Hotel Dusk's usage just felt distinctive. :NoteEnd). Each character connects to numerous others. If this connection is not contained within the literal narrative, it will be done through a narrative parallel. These links are also lax, which assists in making Hotel Dusk's narrative clever and interconnected, but not an overly serendipitous woven ball.

The characters are nuanced and have good material to work with. With the archetypes given, it is easy to imagine the characters becoming frustrating in another game (NoteStart: I had just played Danganronpa before Hotel Dusk and the difference in character writing was astonishing. Angel and Louis in particular. Louis is shockingly tolerable for being a misogynist far too interested in young women, though I'm not thrilled about that at all, but he does suffer from the worst dialogue writing in the game. Near the end of the game, he started to repeat words and phrases in his dialogue ad nauseam. :NoteEnd), but Hotel Dusk succeeded in making troubled characters understandable, sympathetic, and human. Angel in particular is a stand-out.

What is most impressive about Hotel Dusk is that it succeeds in telling a story with barely any catharsis. The narrative wrapped around Bradley, The Nile, and numerous missing people, yet resolutions are not given for these plot lines. Arguably, the hotel backdrop is the key reason why Hotel Dusk is able to pull this off.

Hotels are symbols of luxury and relaxation, while equally communicating drama, intrigue, and fraud. They are also transitional locations, which lends them well to narrative revelations and change. Hotel Dusk cultivates a laid-back and tenuous hotel; it is not luxurious, and it is not for business, it is for interlopers.

Hotel Dusk consistently focused on the discovering the next steps that need to be taken instead of conclusions or solutions. Hyde can help others recognize guilt, but he cannot solve that guilt for them. Hyde himself is only given hints about Bradley, he is given no absolute answers. The Hotel Dusk experience is more akin to wading in and out of water rather than an exhilarating chase. At times the languid pace can be too slow, though that predominantly hinders the opening of the game where the characters and mystery have barely developed.

Hotel Dusk is not mind-bending or life changing, it is mellow to a striking extent. That is a refreshing characteristic for a mystery game.

Gameplay

Exploration is where Hotel Dusk is most troublesome on a mechanical level. It often knows what plot beats it would like to hit, but it struggles in connecting these points or guiding the player to them. The game utilizes a clock framing device: each chapter takes place over an hour, and the chapters are broken up into 20 minute intervals. There are a few instances of characters instructing the player to meet them at a certain time, though this is not taken advantage of as much as it could have.

Most commonly, the player will walk around and interact with elements until they hit an event trigger, or characters will exit their room right as the player walks by to start an event chain. The latter is frequently used, and it comes across as a weak tactic.

Characters do not exist when they are not important for a chapter or subchapter. They seldom sit in public areas, and they will not respond to Hyde knocking on their doors. When Hotel Dusk warps the player back to their room, and then has a character open their door as soon as the player enters the hallway, it takes away from the magic of the writing. In these moments the characters are NPCs who fade in and out of existence depending on game triggers.

The poor utilization of the characters creates a dull exploration environment. New areas open up which adds some variety, but a sizable portion of the game is spent scanning through the same areas with no one to talk to. The emptiness does create a unique atmosphere, and there may have been limitations with regard to the DS's capabilities or the writing staff's resources. Nonetheless, the fragile existance of the NPCs protrudes while playing Hotel Dusk.

The puzzles are cute and simple. Hotel Dusk has two different puzzles that involve closing the DS, which is the best kind of DS puzzle. The second instance is used during one of the greatest CPR 'puzzles' ever, so it does earn points there. At times the puzzles felt more like filler than puzzle, however that is the nature of most DS games.

Hotel Dusk's gameplay climax is the most involved puzzle in the game: a locked room puzzle that properly utilizes its space across numerous pieces. The puzzle is complex and must be completed under a time constraint. The time constraint works well in adding tension to the climax, but it is at odds with the design of the rest of the game. No other puzzle has the same amount of stakes, and Hotel Dusk is tedious to replay after a set back. These issues compound, creating a gameplay scenario that players will be more likely lookup answers for, rather than engage with the puzzle as is. The concept is good, it just could have been assisted with better gameplay build up.

Converstations

The poor exploration and puzzle design are excusable since Hotel Dusk places emphasis onto its exemplary dialogue gameplay. The DS's orientation makes the game "feel" like a novel, but it also serves to center character emotion and converstation.

The characters and dialogue in Hotel Dusk are exciting and have depth because they are not easy to nagivate. Digging into a person's private life requires delicacy, and as such, the player can easily get a game over if they offend the person they are talking to. There can be situations where conversations will end prematurely because the player picked a poor conversation topic and their discussion partner will no longer want to talk with them.

This system is not without frustrations: the game moves at a sluggish pace and there is no way for the player to speed through dialogue if they are set back. Some conversations can be failed by a single wrong choice in a larger conversation chain. The character will react positively, but once it's time for the game to advance they will suddenly change their attitude and the player will receive a game over (NoteStart: A hidden “relationship point” system may have worked better than singular options failing the whole conversation chain, but I do think a single choice should fail a conversation if it is terrible enough. The issue with Hotel Dusk is that in some instances it does not fail the conversation right away and the characters' moods are not consistent as a result. :NoteEnd).

Even with these issues, the conversation system in Hotel Dusk is thrilling because it is novel and well supported by the game's themes. Detective games are fundamentally about talking with people, yet the tone in which the player talks with others rarely matters. Failing typically involves multiple missteps that deplete a health meter (NoteStart: Think Ace Attorney or Danganronpa. :NoteEnd), the words and actions of the player barely matter. In many cases, a character being mad at the player is baked into the plot without any player input.

Consequence does not randomly pop up to punish player actions: it is the principle on which Hotel Dusk operations on. All the characters are reeling from a selfish or misguided action, and that is not something that can necessarily be solved. That is why Hotel Dusk is not cathartic or exhilarating, and why the player cannot speak to characters in any way they would like. Actions have a result, and everyone must sit with that result.
